It's two in the morning, and you're sitting in your home office, looking at a resignation letter you wrote three weeks ago. It's four sentences long. You've never sent it.
You're forty-four. You're a director at a big technology company. You've been promoted five times in fifteen years. This year, your total pay will be about four hundred and fifty thousand dollars.
And you can't quit.
Not because nobody would hire you. Not because you don't want to. You want to more than anything. Your chest has felt tight for six months. Your doctor used the word hypertension last week. Your daughter asked why you're always on your phone at dinner.
You open a second browser tab. Your company's stock plan page. At the top, in big green numbers, it shows your unvested shares. About four hundred thousand dollars. Money you'd leave on the table the moment you walked out the door.
You close the resignation letter. Again. This is the story of how the best job you ever had became the one you can't leave. And how the handcuffs got made, one promotion at a time.

Chapter one. The first promotion.
Fifteen years ago, you were twenty-nine, a software engineer earning a hundred and ten thousand dollars. You rented a small apartment with your wife Leah. You rode your bike to work. You loved the job.
Your father worked thirty-two years at the same factory. He hated the night shifts. But he stayed, because leaving meant giving up his pension. You used to feel sorry for him. You had no idea you were about to walk into the modern version of the same deal.
At thirty-one, your first promotion. Senior engineer. A raise, and something new in your offer letter. Restricted stock units.
RSUs are shares of the company, promised to you, but not all at once. They vest over time, usually over four years. Often with a one-year cliff, which means you get nothing if you leave before your first anniversary. After that, a slice arrives every few months.
It felt like a gift. Free shares, just for staying. And the company's stock kept rising, so every slice was worth more than the last.
What you didn't understand yet was the design. Stock that vests over four years isn't only a reward for the past. It's a reason to stay in the future. Every grant is a small chain. And the company planned to give you a lot of them.

Chapter two. The ladder.
At thirty-three, staff engineer. At thirty-five, engineering manager. At thirty-eight, you switch companies for a senior manager role, with a sixty-thousand-dollar signing bonus. At forty-one, director.
Every step came with more money. More base salary. A bigger bonus. And more stock, stacked on top of the stock you already had.
Every step also came with less of the thing you actually loved. As an engineer, you built things. As a manager, you ran meetings about people who built things. As a director, you ran meetings about meetings.
There's an old idea about this called the Peter principle, from a 1969 book by Laurence Peter. In organizations, people tend to get promoted until they reach a job they're not good at, or don't enjoy. And then they stay there.
You're good at your job. That's not the problem. The problem is that you're good at a job you never wanted, and every promotion made it more expensive to leave.

Chapter three. The four hundred thousand dollar chain.
Here's how the handcuffs work. Every year, your company gives you a new batch of stock, called a refresher. About a hundred and sixty thousand dollars, vesting over four years.
Now picture four of those grants overlapping. The one from four years ago is almost done. The one from three years ago is halfway. Last year's has barely started. And this year's just arrived.
Add up what hasn't vested yet, and it's around four hundred thousand dollars. Always. Every time a slice vests, a new grant fills the hole. The chain never gets shorter.
So there's never a clean moment to leave. If you wait until next quarter, another slice vests, and another grant has started. You're always three months away from a better time to quit.
And stock isn't the only chain. When you switched companies, your sixty-thousand-dollar signing bonus came with a clawback. Leave within two years, and you'd have to pay part of it back. Some companies also make their retirement match vest over several years, so leaving early means losing part of what they put in.
At startups, the chain is often made of stock options instead. The right to buy shares at a fixed price. But many option plans give you only ninety days after you leave to buy them. That can mean writing a check for tens of thousands of dollars, sometimes plus taxes, for shares you can't even sell yet. Plenty of people simply walk away with nothing.
None of it is illegal. None of it is secret. It's all in the paperwork you signed, excited, on your first day.

Chapter four. Why companies pay you in stock.
It wasn't always like this. For most of the twentieth century, the golden handcuff was a pension. Stay thirty years, and the company paid you for life.
Then two things changed. Companies moved away from pensions toward 401k plans, where the risk sits with you. And tech companies discovered that stock was the perfect way to pay people. It cost them little cash today. It made employees think like owners. And it made leaving painful.
In the nineties, the tool was stock options. Then new accounting rules around 2006 made companies count options as an expense. Many switched to RSUs instead. Microsoft had already made that move in 2003. Much of the tech industry followed.
The pension kept your father in place with a promise about the far future. Your stock keeps you in place with a promise about the next three months. Different chain. Same lock.

Chapter five. The life that grew to fit the paycheck.
But the stock is only half the trap. The other half is at home.
At thirty-eight, with two kids and a big raise, you and Leah bought a one point three million dollar house, in the neighborhood where your coworkers lived. You borrowed over a million dollars at six point eight percent. The mortgage alone is about sixty-eight hundred dollars a month. With taxes and insurance, over eight thousand.
Then came two new cars. A nanny, because you both work late. Summer camps. A lake cabin you rent every August. Dinners out, because nobody has the energy to cook.
You add it up one night. Your family spends about nineteen thousand five hundred dollars a month, after taxes. About two hundred and thirty-four thousand dollars a year.
And that number changes everything. Because it means your freedom isn't measured by what you earn. It's measured by what you spend. You don't need a job that pays four hundred and fifty thousand dollars. But you've built a life that does.
Leah sees it before you do. One night, after you cancel a family dinner for a work call, she says it out loud. "I didn't marry a director. I married the guy who rode his bike to work and came home happy."
You don't have an answer. Because she's right, and you both know it.
The company put the first handcuff on your wrist. You put the second one on yourself.

Chapter six. The tax surprise.
Every April, the same thing happens. Your accountant calls. "You owe about twenty-three thousand dollars."
Here's why. When your RSUs vest, they're taxed as ordinary income, just like salary. Companies usually withhold federal tax on them at a flat twenty-two percent. But at your income, your real federal rate on those shares is closer to thirty-five percent.
On a hundred and eighty thousand dollars of stock vesting in a year, that gap is about twenty-three thousand dollars. Money you thought was yours, that was never really yours.
Most companies handle the tax by quietly selling some of your vesting shares for you. It's called sell to cover. You see fewer shares arrive than you were promised, and assume the tax is done. It usually isn't.
And there's a second mistake almost everyone makes. When your shares vest, you keep them. It feels loyal. It feels smart. You work there. You believe in the company.
But now your salary, your bonus, your unvested stock, and most of your savings all depend on one company. If it stumbles, you don't lose one thing. You lose everything at once.

Chapter seven. The Enron lesson.
In 2000, Enron was one of the most admired companies in America. Its stock traded at around ninety dollars a share. Many of its employees had put much of their retirement savings into Enron stock.
By the end of 2001, the company had collapsed in one of the biggest accounting scandals in history. The stock fell to under a dollar. Thousands of employees lost their jobs and much of their retirement savings, at the same time.
They weren't foolish. They trusted the place they worked. That's exactly what made it so dangerous.
Your company isn't Enron. But in 2022, its stock fell forty percent in eight months. Your unvested four hundred thousand became two hundred and forty. Your yearly pay dropped by tens of thousands of dollars, without anyone cutting your salary.
And here's the strangest part. Instead of making you want to leave, it made you want to stay. You couldn't quit now. Not at the bottom. You'd wait for the stock to recover. Just one more year.
Then, in late 2022 and early 2023, the tech industry did something it hadn't done in years. Mass layoffs. Meta cut about twenty-one thousand jobs in two rounds. Google cut about twelve thousand. Amazon, even more.
Some of those people had stayed for years, waiting for their next vest. And when they were laid off, most of their unvested stock simply disappeared. That's the lesson nobody tells you. The handcuffs only work in one direction. They keep you from leaving. They don't keep the company from letting you go.

Chapter eight. The friend who left.
Your friend Marcus started at the company the same year you did. Same team. Same desk row. Same promotions, for the first ten years.
At forty, Marcus quit. He left almost three hundred thousand dollars of unvested stock on the table. Everyone thought he'd lost his mind.
Now he runs a small woodworking shop and teaches evening classes at the community college. He makes about a third of what he used to. You visit him on a Saturday, and the shop smells like sawdust and coffee. "Everybody asks how I could walk away from that much money. Nobody asks how much I was paying to stay."
Marcus did something years before he quit. He sold his shares as they vested, and invested the money in a broad index fund. He paid off his car. He never bought the big house. And he built what he called his walk-away fund. Two years of expenses, in cash and simple investments.
When he quit, he didn't need the next paycheck. That's the only reason he could leave.
You ask him if he was scared. He laughs. "Terrified. For about six months. Then I realized I was sleeping through the night for the first time in years."
He's honest about the hard parts too. His first year, the shop lost money. His wife picked up extra shifts. They sold their second car. Freedom wasn't free. But it was paid for in advance.

Chapter nine. The walk-away number.
That night, you do the math Marcus did.
You have about nine hundred thousand dollars in your 401k. It sounds like a lot. But most of it is locked until you're fifty-nine and a half. Take it out early, and you'll usually pay income tax plus a ten percent penalty. There are some exceptions, like the rule of fifty-five, if you leave your job in the year you turn fifty-five or later. You're forty-four.
Your cash savings? About forty thousand dollars. Two months of your family's life.
So you write down the number that actually matters. To take eighteen months off, or to start something smaller, at your current spending, you'd need about three hundred and fifty thousand dollars, set aside and ready. You have forty.
That's when you finally see it clearly. You're not trapped by the company. You're trapped by the gap between your walk-away number and your walk-away fund. And that gap is something you can close.

Chapter ten. The price of staying.
Before you start closing the gap, you make one more list. Not what you'd lose by leaving. What you're losing by staying.
Blood pressure medication at forty-four. Sleep that ends at three in the morning. Two missed school plays this year. A marriage that's become a shared calendar.
In 2019, the World Health Organization recognized burnout as an occupational phenomenon. Chronic workplace stress that hasn't been successfully managed. Exhaustion. Cynicism. Feeling less effective at work. You check every box.
Your doctor puts it more simply. She looks at your numbers, then at you. "Whatever you're doing, your body is telling you it can't keep doing it."
And then you realize something about the golden handcuffs. They aren't made of gold. They're made of time. Every vesting slice is really a trade. Three more months of your life, for a few more shares.
When you were thirty, that was a good trade. At forty-four, you're not so sure anymore.

Chapter eleven. The eighteen-month plan.
So you and Leah sit at the kitchen table on a Sunday, and make a plan. Not to quit tomorrow. To be able to quit.
Step one. Sell your shares as they vest. Every quarter. No more guessing where the stock is going. Put the money into broad index funds and savings. And set aside extra for taxes, so April stops being a surprise.
Step two. Cut the fixed costs. You sell one of the two new cars. You skip the lake cabin this year. You cook four nights a week. Your spending drops by about four thousand dollars a month.
Step three. Make a vesting calendar. You mark every date when a slice of stock vests and when your bonus pays out. If you leave, you'll leave right after a big one, not right before.
Step four. Talk to other companies, quietly. You learn that many employers will offer a make-whole grant, extra stock to replace some of what you'd give up. The chain isn't as unbreakable as you thought.
You also reread your contract, looking for a non-compete clause. Many workers have signed one without noticing. In 2024, the Federal Trade Commission tried to ban most non-competes nationwide. A federal court in Texas blocked the rule, and it never took effect. But some states, like California, already ban most of them. Yours turns out to be narrow. One more chain that was weaker than it looked.
Eighteen months later, your walk-away fund has three hundred and ten thousand dollars in it. Not quite the full number. But close enough to change how you feel on a Monday morning.

Chapter twelve. The conversation.
And then something unexpected happens. Because you no longer need the job, you stop acting like you do.
You start saying no to meetings that don't matter. You stop answering emails at eleven at night. You tell your boss, honestly, that you're burned out. And instead of firing you, she asks what would help.
You make a proposal. Step down from director to a senior technical role. Back to building things. Fewer meetings. No more direct reports. About thirty percent less pay. "You'd really give up the title?"
You would. And because you have a walk-away fund, she knows you mean it. She agrees.
Thirty percent less pay sounds terrifying at first. About three hundred and fifteen thousand dollars instead of four hundred and fifty. But you've already cut your spending by four thousand dollars a month. You've stopped holding company stock. And your walk-away fund is still there, untouched. For the first time in years, the math works without every promotion you'll ever get.
You didn't quit. But you stopped being trapped. And it turns out that's what you really wanted.

Chapter thirteen. What this means for you.
You don't need stock options or a director title for this to be your story. Handcuffs come in many forms. A pension you're waiting for. A bonus that pays out in March. Or just a mortgage that only works with your current salary.
First. Know your handcuffs. Write down every vesting date, clawback, bonus, and retirement match rule. Most people have never read their own offer letter twice.
Second. Don't let one company own your whole financial life. Many advisors suggest keeping any single stock to a small part of your savings. Your job is already a big bet on your employer. Your savings shouldn't be.
Third. Keep your fixed costs below your future, not your present. Buy the house and the cars you could still afford on a smaller paycheck.
Fourth. Build a walk-away fund. Even six to twelve months of expenses gives you something priceless. The power to say no.
Fifth. Plan for taxes on your stock. If your company withholds less than you'll owe, set extra aside, or adjust your withholding.
Sixth. Count what you're paying to stay. Health. Sleep. Time with your kids. Those don't show up on the stock plan page. But they vest too, and you can't earn them back.
And seventh. Remember the handcuffs are negotiable. New employers can buy out unvested stock. Your current employer can change your role. But you only get to negotiate when you can afford to hear no.

Two years later, it's two in the morning again. But this time, you're not in your office. You're asleep.
In the morning, you find the old resignation letter in a folder on your laptop. Four sentences. You read it once, and smile.
You never sent it. You didn't need to. You work fewer hours, building things again. Your blood pressure is back to normal. You were at every school play this year.
On Saturday, you drive your daughter to Marcus's shop for a woodworking class. She builds a crooked little birdhouse. You build one too. Yours is even more crooked.
She tells you it's the best thing you've ever made. You don't argue.
The stock plan page still shows a number in green. It just doesn't own you anymore. Because the most valuable thing in your portfolio was never the shares. It was the freedom to walk away from them.
