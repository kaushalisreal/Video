This morning, your phone decided your day before you did. Three emails. Two meetings. One boss who writes "quick call?" like it's ever quick. By the time your feet touched the floor, your hours already belonged to someone else. And tonight, you'll plug that phone in, so it's fully charged to ruin tomorrow too.

Now picture a man worth a hundred billion dollars. He gets the same twenty-four hours you do. Not one minute more. He can buy any house, any jet, any company on Earth. But he cannot buy a twenty-fifth hour. Believe me, he's asked.

So what does he actually do with the twenty-four he gets?

Your brain already has an answer, and it's one of two stories. Story one: billionaires do nothing. They float on yachts, sip champagne, and let money fall on them like weather. Story two: billionaires never stop. They wake up at four, drink a smoothie made of kale and ambition, and work a hundred hours a week.

Both stories are wrong. And the proof has been sitting in their calendars the whole time.

So let's look at two calendars. Both belong to men who became among the richest people alive.

The first calendar is packed. Every day is chopped into tiny blocks. Meeting. Call. Review. Meeting about the review. There isn't one empty square. This man schedules his lunch the way you schedule a root canal.

The second calendar is almost blank. Some days have one meeting. Some days have nothing at all. It looks like your calendar. Except he's not unemployed.

So which one belongs to the billionaire? Take a guess.

Most people point to the packed one. Busy means important, right? That's why you answer emails in the bathroom.

But it's the opposite. The packed calendar belonged to Bill Gates, back in his early Microsoft years, when he's said he didn't believe in weekends or vacations. The empty one? Warren Buffett. And Gates says the day Buffett showed him that nearly blank calendar, it changed how he thought about time. The lesson: you control your time. And sitting and thinking can be worth more than a day full of meetings.

Which is great news. You've been sitting and thinking in meetings for years. Just not about the meeting.

So if they're not lounging, and they're not grinding, what does a normal day actually look like? Let me walk you through one.

You wake up. No alarm. You slept eight hours. Jeff Bezos has said this is non-negotiable, because his job is to make a few big decisions. A tired billionaire doesn't make small mistakes. He makes billion-dollar ones. When you're tired, you send an email to the wrong Steve.

You don't touch your phone. You make coffee. You read the paper. You eat breakfast with your kids. Bezos calls this "puttering." Your first serious meeting? Around ten. At ten a.m., you've already been yelled at twice and eaten a granola bar in traffic.

Then comes the part that looks the least like work. And it's the part that matters most. You read. Buffett has said he spends most of his day reading. Newspapers. Annual reports. Hundreds of pages. Asked for the secret, he pointed at a stack of reports and said: read five hundred pages like this every day. That's how knowledge works. It builds up, like compound interest. You read five hundred pages too. Of terms and conditions. Well. You scrolled past them.

And twice a year, for a whole week, Gates would disappear. Just a cabin in the woods, boxes of books, and no phone. He called it Think Week. If you vanished into the woods for a week with no phone, it would be called a missing persons report.

At this point, you're probably thinking: nice job. Sleep in. Read the news. Go camping. Where do I apply?

And honestly? Scientists had the same doubt. So they measured it.

In a Harvard Business School study, Michael Porter and Nitin Nohria tracked twenty-seven CEOs of big companies, twenty-four hours a day, for thirteen weeks. More than sixty thousand hours of data. These leaders worked about sixty-two and a half hours a week. They worked most weekends. They worked most vacation days. So the yacht story? It sank.

But here's what matters: what that work was. Seventy-two percent of their work time was meetings. Not building. Not typing. Talking, listening, and deciding. Basically your job, except when they say "let's circle back," a company actually changes direction.

Bezos says if he makes three good decisions a day, that's enough. A normal job pays you for your hours. At this level, you get paid for judgment. Your last big decision was which streaming service to cancel. And you still haven't cancelled it.

Not every billionaire plays it calm. Elon Musk has said he's worked up to a hundred hours a week, at times sleeping at the factory. But even his hours go to the same thing. Deciding. Which rocket. Which design. Which people. Mostly which people to fire.

And here's the part nobody tells you. The most valuable skill at the top isn't doing more. It's saying no. There's a line often credited to Buffett: really successful people say no to almost everything. Billionaires pay entire teams to guard their calendar. Hundreds of requests come in. Almost all of them die before reaching the boss. You can't even say no to a group chat.

And while they're saying no, their money is saying yes. To everything.

A billionaire doesn't have a billion dollars in a bank account. Almost all of it is stock. Pieces of companies. So on a good day, they can get hundreds of millions richer before lunch, without lifting a finger. On a good day, you find ten dollars in an old jacket.

And when they need cash, they usually don't sell. Selling means tax. So they borrow against their stock. A loan isn't income, so it isn't taxed like a paycheck. The stock keeps growing. And in the US, a rule called the step-up in basis can wipe out the tax on that growth when they die. It's called Buy. Borrow. Die. Regular people do the same three steps. Except you buy sneakers, borrow at twenty-four percent, and the dying is mostly emotional.

In 2021, ProPublica looked at leaked IRS data on the twenty-five richest Americans. Measured against how much their wealth grew, they paid a true tax rate of about three point four percent. The IRS takes your cut before your paycheck even says hello.

Now look around a billionaire's day, and you'll notice something. The chef. The driver. The jet. The security team. Meta has spent more than twenty million dollars a year just protecting Mark Zuckerberg. Your security system is a dog that barks at the mailman and trusts burglars. And then there's the family office, a private company whose only job is managing one family's money. There are now more than eight thousand of them. Your family office is the group chat where your uncle shares fake news.

None of that is really about luxury. It's about time. Every one of those people exists to take something off the billionaire's plate. Buffett once said, "I can buy anything I want, basically, but I can't buy time."

Except, in a way, they can. In 2016, economist Raj Chetty and his team studied over a billion tax records. The richest one percent of American men live about fifteen years longer than the poorest one percent. Fifteen years. Money doesn't just buy nicer years. It buys extra ones.

Then evening comes. Dinner at home. Phones away. In bed before midnight, because tomorrow's three decisions need a clear head. You're in bed before midnight too. On your phone. Until two.

So now you've seen the whole picture. The mornings are slow. The reading is the real work. The meetings are where decisions happen. The word "no" protects everything. And the money keeps working all night.

Now compare that to your day. You'll spend around ninety thousand hours of your life at work. You sell those hours one at a time, and when you stop, the money stops. They own things that earn while they sleep. You rush breakfast so you're not late. They protect breakfast like a board meeting. You fill your calendar to prove you're busy. They empty theirs to prove they're in control.

Nobody handed them twenty-five hours. They got the same twenty-four as you. The difference is who decides what goes inside them.

And tomorrow morning, when your phone lights up and starts deciding your day for you, try asking the question they ask every day.

Whose hours are these?

Then answer "quick call?" anyway. Baby steps.
